Buy an off-the-shelf product when the process it covers is standard and the product fits most of what you need without heavy workarounds. Build custom software when the process is what sets your business apart, when no product fits without bending how you work, or when licence and integration costs grow faster than the value you get. Most organisations end up with a mix of both.

## What each option really means

Off-the-shelf software is a product built for many customers. You configure it, pay a subscription or licence, and accept the vendor's roadmap. Examples include accounting packages, CRMs, help desks and HR systems.

Custom software is built for one organisation. You own the scope, the roadmap and usually the code. You also own the responsibility for maintaining it.

There is a middle ground that is often overlooked: a standard product extended with custom integrations, automations or a small custom application on top. For many teams this is the right first step.

## Where off-the-shelf wins

A product is usually the better choice when the following are true:

- The process is common across companies, such as payroll, bookkeeping or email marketing.
- You need to be live quickly and have little appetite for a build project.
- The vendor carries compliance work you would otherwise carry yourself.
- Your team is small and nobody will own a custom system after launch.
- The product's standard workflow is close to how you already work, or you are happy to adopt it.

Buying also gives you something that is easy to undervalue: the product has already been tested by many other customers. Bugs you would have found the hard way have been fixed.

## Where custom software wins

Custom software earns its cost in a narrower set of situations, but they are important ones.

### The process is your advantage

If the way you quote, schedule, price, route or serve customers is the reason people choose you, forcing it into a generic product removes the advantage. Custom software lets the system follow the process instead of the reverse.

### You are paying for workarounds

Signs include spreadsheets that sit between two systems, staff retyping data, and a growing list of add-ons that each solve a small gap. Each workaround has a cost in time and errors. At some point the total is higher than a system built to fit.

### Integration is the real work

Some businesses run on five or six products that do not talk to each other well. A custom layer that connects them, or replaces two of them, can be simpler than another product.

### Per-seat pricing does not match your model

Licence costs that scale with users can become painful when you have many occasional users, such as field staff, partners or customers. Custom software has a different cost shape: higher at the start, flatter over time.

### You need control over data and roadmap

If you must host in a particular region, meet a specific security requirement, or cannot wait for a vendor to prioritise a feature, ownership matters.

## A side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Off-the-shelf | Custom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to first use | Short | Longer |
| Upfront cost | Low | Higher |
| Ongoing cost | Subscription, often per user | Hosting, maintenance and improvements |
| Fit to your process | Approximate | Exact, if requirements are clear |
| Roadmap control | Vendor decides | You decide |
| Maintenance | Vendor | You, or a partner under a support agreement |
| Switching cost | Data export and retraining | Depends on code quality and documentation |
| Main risk | Lock-in and price rises | Delivery risk and ongoing ownership |

## A decision framework

Work through these five questions in order. They are designed to be answered in a single workshop with the people who run the process.

1. **Is this process a differentiator or a commodity?** If customers would never notice how you do it, lean towards buying.
2. **How well does the best available product fit?** List your must-have requirements and score the top two or three products honestly. A rough rule of thumb, not a statistic: if a product covers most of the must-haves without workarounds, buy it. If the gaps are in the core workflow, keep going.
3. **What does each option cost over several years?** Compare total cost of ownership, not the first invoice. For a product, include licences at your expected headcount, add-ons, integration work and admin time. For custom, include the build, hosting, support and a budget for changes.
4. **Who will own it?** Custom software needs a named owner on the business side and a team, internal or external, that maintains it. If nobody will own it, buy.
5. **What happens if you are wrong?** Check the exit. For a product, confirm you can export your data in a usable format. For custom, confirm you own the code and that it is documented well enough for another team to pick up.

If the answers point in different directions, the hybrid route is usually the safest: keep the product for the commodity parts and build only the piece that is specific to you.

## Costs people forget

Both options have costs that rarely appear in the first comparison.

For off-the-shelf products:

- Configuration and data migration.
- Paid tiers needed for features such as single sign-on, audit logs or API access.
- Integration tools and the time to maintain them.
- Price changes at renewal.

For custom software:

- Requirements and design work before any code is written.
- Hosting, monitoring and security updates after launch.
- Changes as the business evolves.
- The time your own staff spend on reviews, testing and decisions.

A fair comparison puts all of these on one page.

## How to reduce the risk of building

If the framework points to custom, three practices lower the risk considerably. Start with a short discovery phase so scope and estimates rest on evidence. Release a small first version that covers the core workflow, then extend it. Insist on code ownership, documentation and a clear support arrangement from the first day.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is custom software always more expensive than buying?

It is usually more expensive at the start. Over several years the picture can change, especially where licence fees scale with users or where workarounds consume staff time. Compare total cost of ownership for both options before deciding.

### Can we start with a product and move to custom later?

Yes, and it is often sensible. Using a product first teaches you what you really need. Make sure you can export your data, and keep notes on the gaps you hit. Those notes become the requirements for a later build.

### Who owns the code in a custom project?

That depends on the contract, so check it. In most client projects the client should own the code and intellectual property for the work they paid for, with any third-party or open-source components listed clearly.

### What if only one part of our process is unusual?

Build only that part. A small custom application that integrates with standard products is often cheaper and lower risk than replacing everything.
