There are three common ways to engage a software partner. Staff augmentation adds engineers to your own team under your direction. A fixed-scope project delivers a defined result for an agreed price. A retainer reserves a set amount of capacity each month for ongoing work. The right choice depends on how clear the scope is, who will manage the work day to day, and where you want delivery risk to sit.

## The three models at a glance

| | Staff augmentation | Fixed scope | Retainer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| You pay for | People's time | A defined deliverable | Reserved monthly capacity |
| Scope | Open, set by you as you go | Fixed and documented upfront | Flexible within the monthly allowance |
| Who manages the work | You | The partner | Shared, with priorities set by you |
| Delivery risk | Mostly yours | Mostly the partner's | Shared |
| Flexibility | High | Low without a change request | High |
| Budget predictability | Medium: known rate, unknown duration | High, if scope holds | High: known monthly fee |
| Best for | Extending an existing team | Well-defined projects | Ongoing development and support |

## Staff augmentation

In staff augmentation, a partner supplies one or more engineers, designers or testers who work as part of your team. They join your stand-ups, use your tools and take priorities from your managers. You are billed for their time, usually monthly.

### When it fits

- You already have a product team and technical leadership.
- You need a specific skill your team lacks, such as mobile, data engineering or machine learning.
- You need extra capacity for a period without making permanent hires.
- The roadmap changes often and you want direct control over priorities.

### Risks

- **Management load.** The added people need direction, reviews and context. If your leads are already stretched, output will disappoint.
- **Outcome risk stays with you.** You are paying for effort. If the work takes longer than expected, the cost is yours.
- **Onboarding time.** New team members need time to learn the codebase and domain before they are fully productive.
- **Knowledge leaving.** When the engagement ends, undocumented knowledge leaves with the person.
- **Employment rules.** Some jurisdictions have rules on how contractors may be engaged. Take legal advice locally.

## Fixed scope

In a fixed-scope project, both sides agree what will be delivered, by when and for what price before work begins. The partner manages the team and the delivery. Payment is usually tied to milestones.

### When it fits

- The requirements are clear, stable and written down.
- There is a hard budget ceiling or a formal procurement process.
- The work has a natural end, such as an MVP, an integration or a migration.
- You do not have the capacity to manage a development team yourself.

### Risks

- **Scope disputes.** Anything not written in the specification is open to argument. Vague requirements cause most fixed-price conflicts.
- **Change is slow and costly.** New ideas need a change request, a new estimate and approval. This is by design, but it frustrates teams who learn as they go.
- **Contingency is priced in.** A sensible partner includes a margin for uncertainty. The less clear the scope, the larger that margin.
- **Incentive to meet the letter of the specification.** Fixed price rewards finishing what was agreed, not discovering what would be better. Clear acceptance criteria and quality standards reduce this.
- **Upfront effort.** A reliable fixed price needs discovery and requirements work first.

A fixed-scope contract should include the specification, acceptance criteria, milestones, a change control process, a warranty period for defects and a clear statement of what is excluded.

## Retainer

A retainer reserves an agreed amount of a team's time each month for a recurring fee. You bring a prioritised list of work, and the team delivers against it within the allowance. Retainers commonly cover a mix of new features, improvements, maintenance and support.

### When it fits

- The product is live and needs continuous improvement.
- Work arrives steadily but is not large enough to justify a full dedicated team.
- You want the same people to stay familiar with your system.
- You want guaranteed availability instead of waiting for a slot.

### Risks

- **Paying for unused capacity.** In a quiet month you may not use the full allowance. Check whether unused hours roll over and for how long.
- **Drift.** Without clear priorities, a retainer can become a stream of small tasks with no visible progress. A monthly plan and review prevent this.
- **Unclear boundaries.** Agree what counts against the allowance, what happens when it is exceeded, and how urgent issues are handled.
- **Overlap with support.** A development retainer is not automatically a support agreement. If you need response time commitments, they must be stated.

## How to choose

Four questions will usually settle it.

1. **How clear is the scope?** If you can write it down and it is unlikely to change, fixed scope is viable. If it will evolve, choose a time-based model.
2. **Who will manage the work?** If you have technical leadership with time to direct people, staff augmentation works. If not, choose a model in which the partner manages delivery.
3. **Is there an end point?** A defined project suits fixed scope. Continuous work suits a retainer or an augmented team.
4. **What matters most: cost certainty or flexibility?** You cannot maximise both. Fixed scope favours certainty. The other two favour flexibility.

Some typical situations:

| Situation | Usually the best fit |
| --- | --- |
| New product idea, requirements still forming | Paid discovery first, then decide |
| Clearly specified MVP or integration | Fixed scope |
| In-house team short of a specialist skill | Staff augmentation |
| Live product needing steady improvement | Retainer |
| Large programme with shifting priorities | Staff augmentation or a dedicated team |
| Post-launch stabilisation and maintenance | Support agreement or retainer |

## Combining models

The models are not exclusive, and many working relationships move through more than one. A common sequence is a fixed-price discovery phase to define the scope, a fixed-scope build for the first release, and a retainer for improvements and support afterwards. Another is to deliver the core product as a fixed-scope project while augmenting the client's team for an adjacent workstream.

Changing model at a natural boundary, such as a release, is normal. Changing in the middle of a milestone is harder and should be negotiated carefully.

## What to agree in any model

Whichever model you choose, settle these points in the contract:

- Ownership of code and intellectual property.
- Access to repositories, environments and infrastructure accounts.
- Confidentiality and data protection.
- How progress is reported and how often.
- Notice periods and what handover includes.
- How replacements are handled if a team member leaves.
- Who is responsible for testing, deployment and security updates.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is time and materials the same as staff augmentation?

Not quite. Both bill for time. In staff augmentation you manage the people directly. In a time and materials project the partner manages a team and delivery, and you pay for the time used. The billing is similar but the responsibility is different.

### Which model is cheapest?

None is cheapest in every case. Fixed scope gives cost certainty but includes a contingency. Time-based models avoid that margin but leave the total open. The lowest overall cost usually comes from matching the model to how clear the scope is.

### Can we change scope in a fixed-scope project?

Yes, through change control. The change is described, estimated and approved, and the price or timeline is adjusted. Frequent changes are a sign that a time-based model would suit the work better.

### What is a dedicated team?

A dedicated team is a group, often with its own lead, that works only on your product for an extended period. It sits between staff augmentation and a retainer: you set priorities, and the partner handles day-to-day management of the team.
