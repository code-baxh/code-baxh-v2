To ship a product quickly and well, a **small senior agency** is usually the fastest, lowest-risk path. A **freelancer** is cheapest for **narrow, well-defined work**. An **in-house team** is the best long-term investment **once you have product-market fit** and steady funding to hire. The right choice depends on your stage, your budget and how much technical leadership you already have, and many products use all three in turn.

## Agency vs freelancer vs in-house at a glance

| | Freelancer | Agency | In-house team |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Lowest rate; you pay only for hours worked | Higher rate, but design, QA, DevOps and project management are included | Salaries plus benefits, recruiting, tools and management, paid whether or not there is work |
| Risk | High: one person, no review, no cover | Lower: a team, code review, and fixed prices that shift delivery risk to the agency | Hiring risk early; lowest delivery risk once the team is established |
| Speed to start | Fast, often days | Fast once the scope is agreed | Slow: recruiting, notice periods and ramp-up take months |
| Continuity | Fragile; knowledge leaves with the person | Good, if the agency documents and keeps a stable team | Best, as long as people stay |
| Skills coverage | One or two specialties | Full stack: product, design, frontend, backend, mobile, QA, DevOps | Whatever you hire, one role at a time |
| Management overhead | High: you act as project manager and tech lead | Low to medium: the agency runs delivery, you set priorities | High: you need an engineering lead and processes |
| Best for | A defined slice of work | Shipping a product end to end | A durable product team after traction |

## What does each option really cost?

Comparing day rates is the most common mistake. What matters is the total cost of getting working software into production, including your own time.

### Freelancer

You pay for hours, which keeps the bill low for a contained task: a landing page, an integration, a bug that needs a specialist. For a whole product, the hidden costs appear. Someone has to write specifications, review the code, test it, set up hosting and fill the skills the freelancer does not have. If that someone is you, your time is part of the cost.

### Agency

The rate per person is higher, but you are buying a delivery system, not hours: a product lead, design, engineering, QA and DevOps working together, with review built in. Most agencies can quote a fixed price once the scope is written down, which moves the risk of overruns from you to them. A short [discovery phase](/blog/discovery-phase-software-project) is what makes that scope reliable.

### In-house team

Salaries are only the start. In the US, the median annual wage for software developers was 135,980 USD in May 2025, according to the [Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/software-developers.htm). On top of wages, benefits made up 30% of private-industry employers' compensation costs in June 2026 ([BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm)). Add recruiting, equipment, software licenses and management time.

The bigger issue is team size. A product needs frontend, backend, design, QA and infrastructure skills. One developer cannot cover all of them well, so a credible in-house team starts at several hires, and you pay them in quiet months as well as busy ones. That is a good investment once the roadmap is long and certain. Before then, it is a large fixed cost riding on an unproven product.

For what the build itself costs at each stage, see [how much a SaaS product costs](/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-build-a-saas).

## Where does the risk sit?

### Key-person risk

A freelancer who falls ill, takes a bigger client or disappears takes your momentum and much of the knowledge of your codebase with them. An agency can replace a person without stopping the project. An in-house team carries the same risk on a smaller scale, which is why documentation matters in every model.

### Quality risk

A solo developer has no one reviewing their work. Agencies vary widely; the risk is a senior team that sells the project and a junior team that builds it. Our list of [questions to ask a software development agency](/blog/questions-to-ask-software-development-agency) is designed to surface that before you sign.

### Ownership risk

Whoever builds it, make sure you own the code repository, the cloud accounts, the domain and the app store listings from day one, under your own logins. Ownership of the code itself is a contract question. Under US copyright law, work an employee creates as part of their job belongs to the employer, but commissioned work only counts as "made for hire" in specific categories and with a signed agreement, as the [US Copyright Office explains](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf). In practice that means any contract with a freelancer or agency should assign the intellectual property to you in writing. Rules differ between countries, so have a lawyer review the contract.

## How do speed and continuity compare?

A freelancer can often start within days, which is why they suit urgent, contained work. An agency starts once the scope and contract are agreed, then ships at the pace of a full team working in parallel. An in-house team is the slowest to start: writing the role, interviewing, waiting out notice periods and onboarding come before the first feature.

Continuity runs the other way. An established in-house team holds the most context and keeps it, provided people stay. A good agency keeps the same team on your product and leaves documentation behind. A freelancer's knowledge is the hardest to transfer, because it usually lives in one person's head.

## How much management does each one need?

This is the factor founders underestimate most.

- **Freelancer:** you are the project manager, the product owner and often the technical reviewer. If you are not technical, you cannot easily judge the work until something breaks.
- **Agency:** the agency runs delivery: planning, estimates, code review, testing and releases. You own priorities and decisions, typically through a weekly demo and a shared backlog.
- **In-house:** you need someone to hire, lead and retain engineers, set standards and make architecture calls. Without a strong technical lead, a new team drifts.

## Which option fits your stage?

| Stage | Usually the best fit | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Testing an idea | A freelancer or a small agency building a clickable prototype | 2–4 weeks of focused work answers whether the idea holds up, without a hiring commitment |
| Building the first product | A small senior agency on a fixed scope | Full skills coverage and a fixed price for an 8–14 week MVP, with no hiring overhead; see [how long a SaaS MVP takes](/blog/how-long-to-build-saas-mvp) |
| After launch, finding fit | An agency retainer, plus your first technical hire | Steady iteration while you hire someone who will own the product long term |
| Scaling with traction | An in-house core team, with outside specialists | Your team owns the product; partners fill gaps such as AI or mobile |
| Established company, new initiative | Your team for core systems, an agency for the new build | Keeps your engineers on the roadmap while the new product moves in parallel |
| A small, well-defined task | A freelancer | The lowest total cost when the scope fits one person's skills |

The models are not exclusive. Staff augmentation, fixed-scope projects and retainers each suit a different stage of working with a partner; we compare them in [staff augmentation vs fixed scope vs retainer](/blog/staff-augmentation-vs-fixed-scope-vs-retainer).

## A common path: agency first, in-house later

Many founders use an agency to ship the first production version, then hire in-house once the product has traction, and the new team takes over a clean, documented codebase rather than a prototype. Three things make that handover work:

1. **You own everything from day one.** Repository, cloud accounts and third-party services sit under your company, not the agency's.
2. **The codebase is built to be handed over.** A mainstream stack, automated tests, a README that gets a new engineer running, and architecture decisions written down.
3. **There is an overlap.** The agency stays on for a period after your first hires join, answering questions and pairing on changes. A defined [hypercare period](/blog/hypercare-post-launch-support) is a good model for it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an agency or freelancer better for building a SaaS?

For a full SaaS you intend to scale, a small senior agency is usually better: full-stack coverage, senior delivery and continuity. A freelancer fits a narrow, well-defined slice of work at lower cost but higher risk. If you choose a freelancer for the whole product, budget your own time for project management and code review.

### Is it cheaper to hire in-house than to use an agency?

Per hour, often yes. In total, usually not until you have steady, long-term work for a full team. An in-house team carries salaries, benefits, recruiting and management costs whether or not there is work, and one hire rarely covers every skill a product needs. Once the roadmap is long and certain, in-house becomes the better value.

### When should a startup hire its first in-house engineer?

Usually after launch, once customers are using the product and the roadmap is driven by their feedback. The first hire should be someone senior enough to own the codebase and later lead a team. Hiring before the product exists often means paying a team to wait on decisions.

### Can I switch from an agency to an in-house team later?

Yes, and it is a common path. It goes smoothly when you own the repository and accounts, the code uses a mainstream stack with tests and documentation, and the agency overlaps with your first hires for a handover period. Ask about handover before you sign, not when you need it.

### Who owns the code when a freelancer or agency builds it?

It depends on the contract and the law where you operate. Commissioned work is not automatically owned by the client in every country, so the contract should assign the intellectual property to you in writing. Have a lawyer review it, and keep the repository under your own account from the start.

### How do I choose between agencies?

Talk to two or three, ask to meet the people who will do the work, and look at code or products they have shipped. Ask how they estimate, how they handle changes in scope and what you receive at the end. A written scope with milestones is a better signal than the lowest quote.
